# Verb Patterns and Connecting Ideas

A learner can know every common tense and still produce unnatural English if the pieces after the verb do not fit the verb's normal pattern. English verbs behave like words with preferred grammatical neighborhoods. Some are followed by an object, some by an infinitive, some by an `-ing` form, some by a clause, and many allow several patterns with different meanings.

This lesson also introduces the basic ways English joins propositions into larger messages. The goal is not to collect conjunctions. It is to see how speakers turn several small ideas into one coherent line of thought.

## A verb comes with a complementation pattern

A grammar reference uses the term `complement` for a word or phrase that completes the meaning licensed by another element. Put more simply, some verbs open a space after themselves and expect a particular kind of thing to fill it.

`Enjoy` naturally takes an `-ing` form: `I enjoy reading.` `Want` naturally takes a `to`-infinitive: `I want to read.` `Tell` can take a person plus a `to`-infinitive: `She told me to wait.` `Say` normally does not use that same pattern: `She said that I should wait` or `She said to wait` in certain contexts, but not standard `She said me to wait`.

This is why vocabulary and grammar cannot be separated completely. Learning `recommend` means learning that American English naturally allows `I recommend trying it` and `I recommend that you try it`. Standard English does not normally use `I recommend you to try it`.

## The `to`-infinitive: a verb viewed as an intended or conceptual action

The infinitive with `to` appears after many verbs of wanting, planning, deciding, trying, agreeing, refusing, learning, and hoping: `want to leave`, `plan to travel`, `decide to wait`, `hope to see you`.

One useful mental model is that the infinitive often points toward an action as an idea, goal, possibility, or next step rather than presenting it as an activity already being experienced. This is not a universal rule, but it helps explain why verbs of intention strongly favor the infinitive.

After adjectives, the infinitive commonly explains the action associated with the quality: `I'm happy to help`, `It's hard to explain`, `She's ready to leave`. After question words, it can package a decision or problem: `I don't know what to say`, `Can you show me how to use this?`

`Too + adjective + to` means the degree prevents the action: `The box is too heavy to carry alone.` `Adjective + enough + to` means the degree is sufficient: `She's old enough to drive.` The location of `enough` changes with nouns: `enough time`, but `fast enough`.

## The `-ing` form: an activity treated as a thing or process

The `-ing` form can function in several ways. In `She is running`, it participates in the progressive aspect. In `Running is good exercise`, the activity itself functions as a noun-like subject. Traditional grammar often calls this noun-like use a gerund.

After certain verbs, the `-ing` form is the normal complement: `enjoy reading`, `avoid driving`, `finish writing`, `keep trying`, `consider moving`, `suggest waiting`, `mind helping`.

After a preposition, a verb normally appears in `-ing` form because the preposition expects a noun-like complement: `before leaving`, `after eating`, `without saying anything`, `by practicing`, `interested in learning`.

This explains the apparently strange form in `I'm used to working late`. The `to` in `be used to` is a preposition, not the infinitive marker, so `working` follows it. The same is true in expressions such as `look forward to seeing you` and `object to paying extra`.

## When infinitive and `-ing` are both possible

Some verbs allow both forms with little change in meaning: `I like reading` and `I like to read` can both describe a preference. The `-ing` version often feels more like enjoyment of the activity, while the infinitive can sound more like a habit or chosen practice, but context often makes the difference small.

Other verbs change meaning substantially. `Stop smoking` means end the activity of smoking. `Stop to smoke` means stop another activity in order to smoke. `Remember locking the door` means you have a memory of the completed action. `Remember to lock the door` means do not forget the future task.

`Try doing` suggests experimenting with a possible method: `Try restarting the app.` `Try to restart the app` means make an effort to perform the action, perhaps because it is difficult.

`Regret telling you` means you feel sorry about an action already taken. `Regret to tell you` is a formal expression used when the regrettable communication is happening now: `We regret to inform you that your application was unsuccessful.`

These contrasts are not arbitrary exam tricks. The form changes how the action is conceptualized: as an experienced event, an intended action, a purpose, or an experiment.

## Bare infinitives: when English drops `to`

Modal verbs take the base form without `to`: `can go`, `should wait`, `must leave`, `will call`.

`Make` and `let` also commonly take an object plus bare infinitive: `They made me wait`, `Let him speak`. In the passive, `make` changes: `I was made to wait.`

`Help` can appear with or without `to` in American English: `She helped me carry the boxes` and `She helped me to carry the boxes` are both grammatical, though the version without `to` is very common.

Perception verbs such as `see`, `hear`, `watch`, and `feel` can use an object plus bare infinitive to present a complete event: `I saw him cross the street.` With `-ing`, the speaker views the event in progress: `I saw him crossing the street.` The difference is similar to aspect: whole event versus inside the activity.

## Coordination: putting equal ideas side by side

Coordination connects elements of roughly equal grammatical status with words such as `and`, `but`, `or`, `so`, and `yet`.

`I called her, but she didn't answer` joins two complete clauses and presents a contrast. `We can leave now, or we can wait` presents alternatives. `The server was down, so we restarted it` presents a result.

In careful writing, two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction are normally separated by a comma: `I wanted to go, but I was too tired.` If the parts are short and closely connected, the comma may be omitted, but using it consistently helps readability.

Do not use a comma alone to join two independent sentences in formal writing. `I was tired, I went home` is a comma splice. Use a period, semicolon, or conjunction: `I was tired, so I went home`; `I was tired. I went home`; `I was tired; I went home.`

## Subordination: making one idea dependent on another

Subordination creates a relationship in which one clause depends on another. Words such as `because`, `although`, `if`, `when`, `while`, `before`, `after`, `since`, `unless`, and `as soon as` introduce subordinate clauses.

`I left because I was tired` makes tiredness the reason for leaving. `Although I was tired, I stayed` presents the tiredness as a fact that might have led to a different result. `If it rains, we'll stay home` presents rain as a condition.

A subordinate clause cannot always stand alone as a complete standard written sentence. `Because I was tired` leaves the reader waiting for the consequence. In conversation, fragments like this are completely normal when context supplies the rest, but formal prose usually attaches the dependent clause to a main clause.

The same relationship can often be expressed with different grammatical packaging. `I stayed home because it was raining` uses a clause. `I stayed home because of the rain` uses a noun phrase. `Because` takes a clause; `because of` takes a noun phrase or noun-like expression.

## Cause and result

`Because` gives a reason: `We canceled the picnic because it was raining.` `So` gives a result: `It was raining, so we canceled the picnic.` These are two ways of organizing the same real-world relationship, but they put the discourse spotlight in different places.

`Since` and `as` can also mean “because,” especially when the reason is treated as known or less central: `Since you're already here, we can start.` In American speech, `because` is usually the clearest choice when you specifically want to state the cause.

`So ... that` expresses a result caused by degree: `The traffic was so bad that we missed the flight.` With nouns, `such ... that` is common: `It was such a bad storm that schools closed.`

## Contrast and concession

`But` connects a direct contrast: `The apartment is small, but it's comfortable.` `Although` and `though` introduce a concession: `Although the apartment is small, it's comfortable.` The speaker acknowledges one fact while showing that the main result still holds.

Standard formal English does not normally use both `although` and `but` to mark the same relationship: `Although it was late, we stayed`, not `Although it was late, but we stayed`.

`Though` is more conversational and can appear at the end of a sentence in American speech: `It was expensive. I liked it, though.` In that position it acts like a small afterthought meaning “however.”

`Even though` makes the concession stronger because the fact is especially surprising relative to the result: `Even though he was exhausted, he kept working.`

## Conditions: from facts to possibilities

A zero conditional describes a relationship treated as generally true: `If you heat ice, it melts.` The present forms do not mean the situation is happening now; they present a general rule.

A first conditional presents a real future possibility and its likely result: `If it rains tomorrow, we'll stay home.` The `if` clause normally uses a present form, not `will`, because the future relationship is expressed by the construction as a whole.

`Unless` usually means “if not”: `Unless you hurry, you'll miss the train` is close to `If you don't hurry, you'll miss the train.` Because `unless` already contains negative meaning, standard English does not normally add another `not` for the same negation.

`In case` is different from `if`. `Take an umbrella in case it rains` means take it now as a precaution because rain is possible. `Take an umbrella if it rains` means the action of taking the umbrella depends on the rain actually happening.

## Relative clauses: attaching identifying or descriptive information to a noun

A relative clause gives information about a noun: `The woman who called me was my manager.` `Who called me` identifies the woman.

For people, `who` is the most natural subject relative pronoun in ordinary speech and writing: `the person who helped me`. For things, `that` or `which` can be used in many restrictive clauses: `the book that I bought`, `the book which I bought`.

American edited prose often prefers `that` for restrictive clauses and `which` for nonrestrictive clauses, though real usage is not perfectly uniform. A restrictive clause identifies which member of a larger set is meant: `The files that contain errors need to be fixed.` A nonrestrictive clause adds extra information about an already identified referent: `This file, which contains several errors, needs to be fixed.` The commas matter because the meaning differs.

When the relative pronoun is the object, it can often be omitted in a restrictive clause: `The book that I bought` and `The book I bought` are both natural. When it is the subject, it cannot normally disappear: `The person called me` is a different structure from `The person who called me`.

`Whose` expresses possession: `the engineer whose laptop crashed`. It can refer to people and, in modern English, also to organizations or things when natural: `a company whose products are used worldwide`.

## The passive voice: choosing the patient as the grammatical subject

The passive uses a form of `be` plus a past participle: `The window was broken`, `The report was sent`, `The system is updated every night`.

In an active sentence, the subject is normally the doer or source of the action: `Someone broke the window.` In the passive, the affected participant becomes the subject: `The window was broken.` The passive is therefore not just another tense. It is a way to organize information.

Speakers choose the passive when the doer is unknown, obvious, unimportant, deliberately omitted, or less important than the affected thing. `My car was stolen` naturally focuses on the car and the event; the thief is unknown. `The application was approved` focuses on the result, and the approving authority may already be obvious from context.

If the agent matters, `by` can introduce it: `The novel was written by Toni Morrison.` But a passive sentence does not need a `by` phrase.

All the familiar tense and aspect choices can combine with passive voice: `is made`, `was made`, `has been made`, `is being made`, `will be made`. First understand the active event, then decide the time/aspect, then decide whether the active doer or affected participant should be the subject.

## Reported speech: presenting someone else's message from your viewpoint

Direct speech reproduces wording: She said, `"I'm tired."` Reported speech integrates the message into your sentence: `She said that she was tired.`

English often shifts tense backward when the reporting verb is in the past, especially when the reporter adopts a past viewpoint: present becomes past, past may become past perfect, and `will` may become `would`. This is commonly called backshifting.

But backshifting is not a mechanical requirement when the original information is still true or the current viewpoint matters. If Maria says `I live in Seoul` and that remains true, both `Maria said that she lives in Seoul` and `Maria said that she lived in Seoul` can be possible, with different viewpoints. The first keeps the present fact alive; the second reports it from the past conversation frame.

`Say` and `tell` differ in complementation. `Say` normally focuses on the message: `She said that she was tired.` `Tell` normally takes a person: `She told me that she was tired.` Standard English does not normally say `She said me` or `She told that...` without an object in the ordinary reporting pattern.

Reported questions use statement word order after the question word: `He asked where I lived`, not `He asked where did I live`. Yes/no questions use `if` or `whether`: `She asked whether I was ready`.

## Prepositions are relational words, not one-to-one translations

Prepositions such as `in`, `on`, `at`, `to`, `for`, `from`, `with`, `by`, and `about` express relationships of space, time, direction, means, association, and more abstract connections.

Their meanings form networks rather than single translations. `At` often presents a point: `at the station`, `at 3:00`. `On` often presents contact with a surface or a day/date: `on the table`, `on Monday`. `In` often presents containment or a larger time period: `in the room`, `in July`, `in 2026`.

These are prototypes, not perfect rules. Natural combinations are partly conventional, so learn important prepositions together with the words they belong to: `interested in`, `responsible for`, `depend on`, `listen to`, `good at`, `afraid of`.

American and British English differ in some common choices. Americans usually say `on the weekend`, while British speakers commonly say `at the weekend`. Americans say `different from` most often in careful usage, though `different than` is also well established in some constructions. British English also widely uses `different to`.

## Phrasal verbs are ordinary verbs with particles that reshape meaning

A phrasal verb combines a verb with a particle such as `up`, `out`, `off`, or `on`: `give up`, `find out`, `turn off`, `carry on`.

The particle may contribute a recognizable spatial metaphor, but many combinations become idiomatic. `Find out` means discover, not physically find something outside. Because the combination behaves as a lexical unit, it should often be learned like a word rather than translated one part at a time.

Some phrasal verbs are separable: `turn off the light` and `turn the light off`. With a pronoun object, separation is required: `turn it off`, not `turn off it`.

Others are inseparable: `look after the child`, not `look the child after`. This is another example of complementation. Natural English depends on the pattern attached to the lexical item.

## Coherence is the real goal

Grammar is not successful merely because each sentence is individually correct. A reader also needs to understand how one sentence relates to the next.

English uses conjunctions, pronouns, repeated key terms, tense consistency, and information order to create that continuity. If one sentence introduces `a problem`, the next may say `the problem`, then later `it`. If a paragraph begins in a past narrative frame, sudden movement into present tense should have a reason. If a sentence starts with `although`, the reader expects a main clause that completes the concession.

At this stage, you should be able to build a basic English message from the inside out. Start with the participants and the verb. Decide how the noun phrases are identified. Choose the time and aspect. Add modality if the proposition is not a plain fact. Then connect the idea to other ideas with coordination, subordination, relative clauses, or information links.

The intermediate lessons build on exactly this architecture. They do not replace it with a new grammar. They show how the same systems become more precise when you express counterfactual situations, past deductions, complex reporting, reduced clauses, and different registers.
