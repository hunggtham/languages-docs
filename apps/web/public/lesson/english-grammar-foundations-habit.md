# Habit, Familiarity, Preference, and Advice

Some of the hardest English structures are not difficult because the grammar is complicated. They are difficult because several short everyday expressions look similar while doing completely different jobs. `used to`, `be used to`, `get used to`, `would`, `would rather`, `prefer`, and `had better` all contain familiar words, but native speakers treat them as different grammatical constructions with different meanings.

This lesson connects them through one idea: English often uses small grammatical patterns to show how a speaker relates an action to time, familiarity, preference, or consequence. The goal is not to memorize seven formulas. It is to understand what viewpoint each construction creates.

## `used to + base verb` presents a past habit or state that is no longer current

`I used to live in Busan.`

`She used to play tennis every weekend.`

`There used to be a bookstore here.`

The construction places a situation in the past and normally suggests contrast with the present. `I used to live in Busan` naturally implies that I do not live there now.

This contrast is part of the construction's normal meaning. A simple past sentence such as `I lived in Busan` only places the situation in the past. It does not automatically tell the listener whether the situation later changed.

`Used to` works with repeated actions and with states. This is important because `would` can also describe repeated past actions, but it does not normally replace `used to` with states.

`When I was a child, I used to be shy` is natural.

`When I was a child, I would be shy` does not normally mean the same thing. It can work in special contexts, but not as the ordinary way to describe a former state.

## Negative and question forms use `did`

In contemporary standard English, the usual forms are:

`I didn't use to drink coffee.`

`Did you use to work here?`

Once `did` carries the past tense, `use` returns to its base form. This follows the same principle you learned with ordinary past questions: `Did she work?`, not `Did she worked?`

You may encounter spellings such as `didn't used to`, especially because pronunciation can make `use to` and `used to` sound nearly identical. In careful edited English, `didn't use to` and `Did ... use to?` are the safer standard forms.

## `would` can describe repeated past behavior, but it needs a past frame

In a narrative context, `would + base verb` can describe actions that happened repeatedly:

`Every summer, we would stay with my grandparents.`

`After dinner, my father would sit outside and read.`

This `would` does not express a hypothetical result. It is a past-habit construction.

It usually works best after the past time frame has already been established. If someone begins a conversation with `I would walk to school`, the listener may initially interpret `would` as conditional. `When I was a kid, I would walk to school every day` is much clearer.

The distinction from `used to` is partly about function. `Used to` efficiently establishes a former habit or state and often highlights present contrast. Habitual `would` is especially natural for repeated actions inside a story or remembered routine.

`We used to visit my grandparents every Sunday` presents the old habit as a fact about that period.

`Every Sunday, we would drive across town, buy pastries, and visit my grandparents` feels more narrative. It walks the listener through a recurring scene.

## `be used to` is a completely different construction

`I'm used to the noise.`

`She's used to working late.`

`We're used to cold winters.`

Here `used` means familiar or accustomed. The construction does not describe a past habit. It describes a current state of familiarity.

The `to` here is a preposition, not the infinitive marker. That is why a following verb takes `-ing`:

`I'm used to getting up early.`

not `I'm used to get up early.`

A useful test is to replace the complement with a noun. `I'm used to the schedule` is clearly possible, which shows that `to` behaves like a preposition.

This construction can appear in different tenses:

`I wasn't used to the weather.`

`You'll be used to it soon.`

`I've become used to working remotely.`

The time comes from `be` or another linking verb. The core meaning remains familiarity.

## `get used to` presents the process of becoming familiar

`I'm getting used to the new system.`

`It took me a while to get used to driving in Seoul.`

`You'll get used to speaking English at work.`

`Be used to` describes the state after adaptation. `Get used to` describes movement toward that state.

Think of the contrast this way:

`I'm used to it` means the adjustment has already happened.

`I'm getting used to it` means the adjustment is still happening.

`I can't get used to it` means the process of adaptation is not succeeding.

The grammar after `to` remains the same because `to` is still a preposition: `get used to working`, `get used to the schedule`, `get used to it`.

## `would rather` expresses a preference for one immediate option

`I'd rather stay home tonight.`

`She'd rather wait until tomorrow.`

`Would you rather drive or take the train?`

The pattern uses `would rather + base verb`. There is no `to` before the main verb.

`I'd rather leave now` means that, given the current choice, leaving now is the option I prefer.

This often feels more immediate and conversational than a neutral statement with `prefer`.

`I prefer working from home` can describe a general preference.

`I'd rather work from home today` sounds like a choice in the current situation.

The distinction is not absolute, but it is useful. `Prefer` can state a stable preference. `Would rather` often presents a choice relative to the present decision or imagined alternative.

## Negative `would rather` places `not` before the main verb

`I'd rather not talk about it.`

`We'd rather not wait.`

`She'd rather not say.`

This is one of the most natural polite ways in American English to decline an action without sounding excessively formal.

`I'd rather not` can even stand alone when the action is understood:

`Do you want to discuss it now?`

`I'd rather not.`

The omitted verb phrase is recoverable from context, so conversational ellipsis makes the short response complete enough for the interaction.

## `would rather + subject + past form` changes who performs the preferred action

Compare:

`I'd rather leave now.`

with:

`I'd rather you left now.`

In the first sentence, I prefer my own leaving. In the second, I prefer your leaving.

The past form `left` does not place the preferred action in past time. It creates modal distance, similar to the past morphology used in counterfactual expressions such as `If I knew` or `I wish you were here`.

`I'd rather you didn't tell anyone` means that my preference is for you not to tell anyone now or later.

This structure is therefore another example of past morphology expressing remoteness or non-asserted reality rather than literal past time.

## `prefer` has several common patterns

`I prefer coffee to tea.`

`I prefer working alone.`

`I prefer to work in the morning.`

`I'd prefer to wait.`

With two nouns, `prefer X to Y` is the standard comparison: `I prefer trains to buses`.

With activities, both `prefer doing` and `prefer to do` are common, though context can influence which sounds more natural. `I prefer working at night` easily describes a general habit or preference. `I prefer to work at night` is also standard and may sound slightly more like a chosen routine.

`Would prefer` commonly takes a `to`-infinitive and is useful for a specific situation:

`I'd prefer to meet tomorrow.`

This is close to `I'd rather meet tomorrow`, but the grammar differs: `would prefer to + verb` versus `would rather + base verb`.

## `rather than` contrasts alternatives without being the same as `would rather`

`I'd rather walk than wait for the bus.`

`Rather than wait for the bus, we walked.`

The first sentence uses the preference construction `would rather X than Y`.

The second uses `rather than` as a contrastive expression meaning approximately “instead of.” It can introduce an alternative action or choice without expressing someone's personal preference in the same grammatical way.

Keeping these constructions separate prevents a common error: the word `rather` does not have one fixed grammar everywhere it appears.

## `had better` gives strong practical advice because a consequence is expected

`You'd better leave now.`

`We'd better check the backup.`

`She'd better call the bank.`

`Had better` is a semi-modal expression. It is followed by the base verb without `to`.

Despite the word `had`, it usually refers to the present or future, not the past. `You'd better leave now` means that leaving now is advisable because some negative consequence may follow if you do not.

This makes `had better` stronger and more situation-specific than ordinary `should`.

`You should back up your files` sounds like general advice.

`You'd better back up the files before running this update` suggests a more immediate risk or consequence.

## `had better` can sound forceful when used directly with people

Because it implies consequences, `You'd better...` can sound like a warning or even a threat depending on tone and context.

`You'd better be careful` may be friendly advice.

`You'd better not do that again` can sound strongly confrontational.

Native-like use therefore depends on pragmatics, not only grammar. In professional situations, `You may want to...`, `It would be better to...`, `I recommend...`, or `We should...` may be socially safer when you do not intend a warning.

This is an important American-English lesson: grammatical strength and social strength often travel together.

## `had better not` places negation after `better`

`You'd better not forget.`

`We'd better not be late.`

`He'd better not touch that.`

The construction is `had better not + base verb`.

The negative does not use `don't` or `didn't`. `Had better` behaves as a fixed semi-modal frame rather than as ordinary lexical `have`.

## `should`, `ought to`, and `had better` are not identical

`Should` is the broad everyday form for advice, expectation, and weak obligation:

`You should get more sleep.`

`The package should arrive tomorrow.`

`Ought to` overlaps heavily with `should`, especially in advice and expectation:

`You ought to get more sleep.`

In contemporary American conversation, `should` is much more frequent and normally sounds more natural. `Ought to` is fully grammatical but can feel more marked, old-fashioned, or formal depending on context.

`Had better` is narrower. It usually implies that the recommendation is tied to a real consequence in the current situation.

`You should take an umbrella` is advice.

`You'd better take an umbrella; the storm is already moving in` adds urgency and consequence.

## `be supposed to` expresses expectation, rule, plan, or social arrangement

`I'm supposed to meet her at six.`

`You're supposed to wear a badge in this building.`

`The train is supposed to arrive at 8:10.`

This construction is useful because it covers a different kind of modality from `should` or `had better`. It often refers to what is expected according to a plan, rule, schedule, or shared understanding.

`You should submit the form today` is advice or recommendation.

`You're supposed to submit the form today` suggests that today's submission is already the rule or arrangement.

In American conversation, `supposed to` is extremely common and is often pronounced in a reduced way, but standard writing keeps the full spelling.

## `was supposed to` often implies that the expected event did not happen

`I was supposed to call you.`

`The meeting was supposed to start at nine.`

`He was supposed to send the file yesterday.`

Context often creates an implication of failure or change: the call did not happen, the meeting started late, or the file was not sent. But the construction itself primarily expresses the prior expectation. Context supplies whether reality matched it.

This is similar to many grammar choices in the curriculum: the form establishes a viewpoint, while pragmatic inference adds the rest.

## These forms are connected by viewpoint, not by vocabulary

`I used to work nights.`

`I'm used to working nights.`

`I'm getting used to working nights.`

`I'd rather work nights.`

`I prefer working nights.`

`I'd better work tonight.`

The repeated words can tempt learners to translate each sentence word by word. That approach hides the grammar.

The first sentence describes a former pattern. The second describes current familiarity. The third describes adaptation. The fourth expresses a situational preference. The fifth expresses preference more generally. The sixth gives practical self-advice tied to the present situation.

Native speakers recognize these as different constructions immediately. The fastest path to that same intuition is to connect each construction to its communicative job.

## American and British English mostly share these structures

`Used to`, `be used to`, `get used to`, `would rather`, `prefer`, and `had better` are standard in both American and British English.

The main regional difference in this area is frequency and conversational preference rather than grammatical availability. Contemporary American English heavily favors `should` over `ought to` in ordinary speech. Both varieties use `would rather`, though alternatives such as `would sooner` are less common and can feel literary or old-fashioned in modern American conversation.

The production target for this curriculum remains straightforward: choose the form a contemporary American speaker would normally use, while recognizing standard alternatives when you encounter them internationally.

## What to carry into the core lessons

When two expressions look similar, do not begin by comparing individual words. Identify the meaning dimension first.

Are you talking about a former state, a repeated past action, present familiarity, the process of adaptation, a general preference, an immediate choice, an expected rule, ordinary advice, or a warning tied to consequences?

Once that relationship is clear, the grammar becomes much easier to choose. `used to`, `would`, `be used to`, `get used to`, `prefer`, `would rather`, `should`, `be supposed to`, and `had better` are not a pile of exceptions. They are compact ways of locating an action relative to time, familiarity, preference, expectation, and consequence.
