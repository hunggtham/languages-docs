# Voice, Reporting, Relative Clauses, and Clause Compression

Intermediate grammar becomes much easier when you see that many “advanced structures” are really choices about where to place information. Passive voice changes which participant becomes the subject. Relative clauses attach information to a noun. Participial clauses compress a clause when the relationship is clear. Reporting structures let one speaker package another person's statement inside a new sentence.

These systems all answer the same broad question: how can English reorganize information without changing the underlying event more than necessary?

## Active and passive voice describe the same event from different grammatical centers

`The engineer fixed the bug` is active. `The bug was fixed by the engineer` is passive. The real-world event is the same, but the sentence chooses a different subject.

In active voice, the doer or source of the action commonly appears as subject. In passive voice, the affected participant becomes subject. The passive is formed with an appropriate form of `be` plus a past participle: `is fixed`, `was fixed`, `has been fixed`, `is being fixed`, `will be fixed`.

The reason to use passive voice is therefore informational, not decorative. `Someone stole my laptop` focuses on the unknown thief as grammatical subject even though the speaker cannot identify that person. `My laptop was stolen` naturally puts the known and important item first.

Technical and professional writing often uses the passive when the process or result matters more than the actor: `The request is validated before it is stored.` This does not automatically make the writing better. If the actor matters, active voice is clearer: `The API gateway validates the request before the service stores it.`

## The agent with `by` is optional

A passive clause can include the agent in a `by` phrase: `The novel was written by Toni Morrison.` But most passive sentences omit the agent when it is unknown, obvious, irrelevant, or intentionally backgrounded.

`He was arrested last night` usually does not need `by the police` because the expected agent is obvious. `The decision was made yesterday` may omit the decision makers because the writer wants to foreground the decision itself.

If the identity of the actor is the most important new information, active voice is often more natural: `The CEO approved the plan` usually places stronger focus on the CEO than `The plan was approved by the CEO`.

## Passive voice can hide responsibility, so use it consciously

`Mistakes were made` is grammatically complete but does not identify who made them. That can be useful when the actor is genuinely unknown or unimportant, but it can also be rhetorically convenient when a writer wants to avoid assigning responsibility.

Good grammar study therefore includes discourse awareness. Passive voice is not “formal English.” It is a tool for controlling topic and focus. In clear American professional writing, choose it when the affected entity or process deserves the subject position, not simply because the sentence sounds more serious.

## Get-passives are more conversational and eventful

American English often uses `get + past participle` in informal contexts: `He got fired`, `My phone got stolen`, `They got stuck in traffic`.

The `get` passive tends to emphasize the event, change of state, or the subject's involvement. `He was fired` is neutral and standard. `He got fired` feels more conversational and event-focused.

Some `get + participle` combinations are better analyzed as resulting states rather than straightforward passives: `get tired`, `get married`, `get dressed`. The practical point is that `get` often highlights movement into a new state.

## Causative `have` and `get`: arranging for someone else to do the action

`I repaired my car` means I performed the repair myself unless context says otherwise. `I had my car repaired` means I arranged for another person to do it.

The causative pattern `have + object + past participle` is common in service contexts: `I had my hair cut`, `We had the server inspected`, `She had the documents translated`.

`Get + object + past participle` expresses a similar idea and is often more conversational: `I got my phone fixed yesterday.`

`Have + person + base verb` can mean cause or arrange for a person to do something: `I'll have Alex call you.` `Get + person + to-infinitive` is also common: `I'll get Alex to call you.` `Get` often suggests persuasion, effort, or successful arrangement more strongly than `have`.

## Reporting verbs change how a message is framed

`Say` and `tell` are only the beginning. English has many reporting verbs that add stance: `claim`, `admit`, `deny`, `suggest`, `recommend`, `warn`, `promise`, `refuse`, `agree`, `insist`, `explain`, `mention`, `announce`.

The grammar after each verb matters. `He admitted making a mistake` and `He admitted that he had made a mistake` are natural. `She warned me not to touch it` uses person + infinitive. `They suggested waiting` or `They suggested that we wait` are natural. `They suggested us to wait` is not standard.

Learning a reporting verb therefore means learning its complementation and its stance. `Claim` can signal that the reporter is not fully endorsing the statement: `The company claims that the data is secure.` `Admit` presents the information as something reluctantly acknowledged: `He admitted that he had ignored the warning.`

## Reported statements are viewpoint changes

Direct speech preserves the original speaker's wording: Maya said, `"I can't come today."` Reported speech integrates the content into another speaker's grammar: `Maya said that she couldn't come that day.`

Tense may backshift because the reporting frame is in the past. Pronouns, time words, and place words may also change because the new speaker has a different point of view.

But these shifts follow meaning, not a transformation machine. If it is still the same day, `Maya said she can't come today` can be perfectly natural in American English. If the fact remains true, present tense can remain present: `The instructor said that water boils at 100°C at sea level`.

The reporter chooses whether to preserve current relevance or to place the whole message inside the past frame.

## Reported questions return to statement word order

A direct question says `Where does she work?` A reported question says `He asked where she worked.` There is no `does` and no subject-auxiliary inversion inside the embedded clause.

`What time is it?` becomes `She asked what time it was.` `Did they leave?` becomes `He asked whether they had left` or, depending on viewpoint, `whether they left`.

For yes/no content, `if` and `whether` are both common: `She asked if I was ready`, `She asked whether I was ready`. `Whether` is preferred in some formal environments and where alternatives are explicit: `We need to decide whether to stay or leave`.

## Relative clauses attach a proposition to a noun

`The woman who called me works in HR` contains the proposition `the woman called me`, but instead of making it a separate sentence, English attaches it to `the woman`.

The noun being described is called the antecedent. The relative clause identifies or comments on that antecedent.

`Who` is natural for people. `Which` is natural for things and can introduce nonrestrictive information. `That` is common in restrictive clauses for people or things, especially in American English: `the person that called`, `the file that failed`.

In careful American editing, `that` is often preferred for restrictive clauses and `which` for nonrestrictive clauses. This is a style preference rather than an absolute fact of the language, but following it produces clear American prose.

`The servers that failed were restarted` means only the failed servers were restarted. `The servers, which failed, were restarted` presents failure as extra information about an already identified set of servers. The commas therefore change the information structure and can change the factual meaning.

## Object relatives can omit the relative pronoun

`The book that I bought` can become `The book I bought` because `that` is the object of `bought`. The subject `I` remains visible, so the relationship is recoverable.

When the relative pronoun is the subject, omission is not normally possible. `The person who called me` cannot become `The person called me` without changing the sentence structure and meaning.

In very formal English, prepositions can move before `whom` or `which`: `the person to whom I spoke`, `the system on which the service depends`. Ordinary American English usually prefers preposition stranding: `the person I spoke to`, `the system the service depends on`. The formal forms are important to recognize, but forcing them into conversation can sound stiff.

`Whom` itself is declining in everyday American speech. `Who did you talk to?` is much more natural conversationally than `To whom did you talk?` In formal writing after a fronted preposition, `whom` remains standard.

## `Whose` is not limited to people

`Whose` expresses a possessive relationship: `the employee whose laptop was stolen`. Modern standard English also uses it with organizations and inanimate entities when the phrasing is natural: `a company whose products are sold worldwide`, `a system whose design makes scaling difficult`.

Avoiding `whose` for things can produce clumsy alternatives. Natural English values clarity more than an outdated rule that possession must belong only to people.

## Reduced relative clauses compress predictable material

`People who live in large cities often pay higher rent` can be compressed to `People living in large cities often pay higher rent`.

The `-ing` form works when the relative clause is active and the time relationship is appropriate. `The data that was collected last week` can become `The data collected last week` because the passive `that was` is recoverable.

Reduction works best when the missing relationship is obvious. `Students wanting extra practice can use this exercise` is clear. But aggressive reduction can create ambiguity or an unnaturally dense noun phrase.

The point is not to make every sentence shorter. Clause reduction is a way to package secondary information more compactly once the reader can recover the full relationship without effort.

## Participial clauses compress relationships between clauses

`Walking home, I saw an old friend` compresses something like `While I was walking home, I saw an old friend.` The understood subject of `walking` is `I`, the subject of the main clause.

This subject relationship is crucial. `Walking home, the rain started` literally makes `the rain` the understood walker, creating a dangling participle. Native readers can often infer the intended meaning, but careful writing should say `While I was walking home, it started to rain` or restructure the sentence.

Past participles often express passive or resulting relationships: `Built in 1920, the house still has its original windows.` The house was built; it did not build something.

Perfect participles mark earlier completion: `Having finished the report, she went home.` This is more compressed and formal than `After she finished the report, she went home`.

## Reduced time and condition clauses

English can also omit a repeated subject and form of `be` in certain subordinate clauses: `When asked about the delay, he refused to comment`; `If necessary, restart the service`; `While in New York, she met several clients`.

The full relationships are something like `When he was asked...`, `If it is necessary...`, and `While she was in New York...`.

These reductions are common in formal, instructional, and professional writing. They are concise because the omitted material is highly predictable. In casual speech, full clauses are often more natural.

## Noun clauses turn a proposition into a sentence part

In `I know that she left`, the clause `that she left` functions as the object of `know`. In `What he said surprised me`, `what he said` functions as the subject.

A noun clause packages an entire proposition so it can occupy a noun-like position. This lets English reason about facts, questions, beliefs, and possibilities as units.

`That` can often be omitted after common reporting and thinking verbs in conversation: `I think she's right`, `He said he was busy`. In formal or complex writing, retaining `that` can improve clarity when clause boundaries would otherwise be difficult to process.

`Whether` clauses package alternatives: `Whether we leave now depends on the weather.` `What`, `why`, `how`, `where`, and other wh-words can create embedded content: `I understand why she left`, `Show me how this works`.

## Extraposition with dummy `it`

Long clause subjects can feel heavy at the beginning of a sentence. English often moves them to the end and inserts dummy `it` in subject position.

`That the project failed is obvious` is grammatical but formal and heavy. `It is obvious that the project failed` is much more natural.

`To learn a language takes time` is possible; `It takes time to learn a language` is ordinary English. The second version lets the listener process a simple subject first and receive the heavier informational content later.

This tendency toward lighter material before heavier material becomes increasingly important in advanced information structure.

## Compression must preserve recoverability

Advanced-looking English is not good English if the reader has to reconstruct too much missing structure.

`The report submitted yesterday by the consultant hired by the client contains errors discovered during testing` is grammatically possible, but the noun phrase has become overloaded with compressed clauses. Breaking it into two or three sentences may be clearer.

Native professional writing constantly balances compression against processing cost. Relative clauses, participial clauses, passive voice, and noun clauses are tools for organizing information, not a contest to remove the most words.

## The main mental model

Start with the underlying propositions. Decide which participant should be the grammatical subject. Decide which proposition deserves its own main clause and which can become supporting information. Use relative clauses to attach information to nouns, passive voice to change the grammatical center, reporting structures to embed someone else's message, and participial reductions only when the omitted relationships remain obvious.

Once you see these structures as information packaging, they stop feeling like separate chapters of unrelated grammar.
