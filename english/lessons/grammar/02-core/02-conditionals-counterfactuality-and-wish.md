# Conditionals, Counterfactuality, and Wish

Conditionals are often taught as numbered formulas: zero, first, second, third, mixed. The formulas are useful, but they become much easier when you see the deeper system. A conditional sentence compares a condition with a consequence, and the grammar shows how the speaker views the condition: as a general truth, a real possibility, a remote hypothetical situation, or a situation contrary to known reality.

The key idea is distance. English often uses past-looking forms not because the speaker is talking about past time, but because past morphology can create distance from present reality. The same distancing principle appears in polite requests with `could` and `would`, in wishes, and in hypothetical clauses.

## Real conditions: rules, habits, and open possibilities

`If water reaches 100°C at sea level, it boils` describes a general relationship. Both clauses use present forms because the sentence presents a rule rather than one particular future event.

`If it rains tomorrow, we'll stay home` describes a future possibility that the speaker treats as genuinely open. English usually uses a present form in the `if` clause even though the meaning is future. The future result can use `will`, a modal, an imperative, or another appropriate form: `If you finish early, call me`; `If the traffic is bad, we may be late`; `If you see Anna, tell her I called`.

The important contrast is not “zero versus first” as labels. The real contrast is general relationship versus one open condition in a particular situation.

## Remote present or future conditions

`If I had more time, I would exercise every day` uses a past form in the condition, but `had` refers to the present. The speaker does not have enough time now and imagines an alternative reality.

Traditional grammar calls this the second conditional. A more revealing description is a remote conditional: the speaker places the condition at a distance from current reality or likelihood.

This is why `If I were you, I'd apologize` uses `were`, especially in careful standard English. The speaker obviously cannot literally become the listener, so the form marks a hypothetical identity. In casual American speech, `If I was you` is widely heard, but `If I were you` remains the standard form expected in formal writing and is common in educated speech.

Remote grammar does not always mean “almost impossible.” `If I won the lottery, I'd travel for a year` may be unlikely. `If I had tomorrow off, I'd go hiking` could be quite realistic but is presented as contrary to the speaker's current understanding. The grammatical distance is about stance, not a numerical probability.

## Counterfactual past conditions

`If I had studied harder, I would have passed` imagines a different past. The speaker now knows that the condition was not fulfilled and the result did not occur.

The `had + past participle` form places the imagined condition before the past reference point. The `would have + past participle` form gives the unrealized consequence.

This structure is not merely a “third conditional formula.” It is how English builds a counterfactual past model: reality took path A; the speaker imagines path B and its consequence.

`If we had left earlier, we wouldn't have missed the train` carries an implicit factual background: we did not leave earlier, and we did miss the train.

## Mixed conditionals follow the time relationship, not a special exception list

Sometimes the unreal condition and consequence belong to different times. `If I had accepted that job, I would live in New York now` imagines a different past decision with a present consequence.

The condition uses past perfect because the decision belongs to the unreal past. The result uses `would + base verb` because the consequence belongs to the unreal present.

The opposite direction is also possible: `If I were more organized, I wouldn't have missed yesterday's deadline.` Here a general present characteristic is imagined as different, and that alternative characteristic would have changed a past result.

Instead of memorizing mixed-conditional types, locate each half independently. What time does the unreal condition belong to? What time does the imagined result belong to? Then choose the form that expresses each viewpoint.

## `Would` normally belongs in the result, not the ordinary `if` condition

Standard conditional English usually says `If I knew, I would tell you`, not `If I would know, I would tell you`.

The reason is structural. The `if` clause establishes the condition, and `would` in the main clause expresses the hypothetical consequence. Putting `would` in the condition often duplicates the same modal job.

There are legitimate exceptions when `would` has an independent meaning such as willingness or repeated insistence. `If you would wait here, someone will help you` uses `would` as a polite willingness expression. `If he would just listen, we could solve this` can express frustration about his unwillingness to listen. These are not ordinary neutral conditional markers.

## `Even if`, `only if`, `unless`, and `as long as`

`Even if` says that the result will remain the same despite the condition: `Even if it rains, we're going.` Rain does not control the decision.

`Only if` makes the condition necessary: `You can enter only if you have a badge.` Without the badge, entry is not permitted. This is logically stronger than a simple `if` sentence.

`Unless` usually means `if not`: `Unless we leave now, we'll be late.` It works best when one negative condition blocks the result. It is less natural when the meaning requires a positive alternative or a complex exception.

`As long as`, `provided that`, and `providing that` all express a condition that must be satisfied: `You can borrow the car as long as you're careful.` `Provided that` is more formal and common in written rules, agreements, and professional contexts.

`On condition that` is even more explicit and formal: `The data may be shared on condition that all personal identifiers are removed.`

## `In case` is precaution, not ordinary condition

`Take a charger in case your battery dies` means take the charger now because battery failure is possible. The precaution happens before we know whether the condition will occur.

`Take a charger if your battery dies` would mean wait until the battery dies and then take a charger, which is usually not the intended idea.

American speakers use `just in case` very frequently to emphasize precaution: `I'll bring an umbrella just in case.` The condition may be omitted when context makes it obvious.

## `Suppose`, `supposing`, and `what if` create imagined worlds

`Suppose you lost your job tomorrow. What would you do?` invites the listener to enter a hypothetical scenario. `Supposing` works similarly but is somewhat less common in everyday American speech.

`What if` is highly natural in conversation: `What if the plan doesn't work?` It can express genuine problem-solving, worry, challenge, or curiosity depending on tone.

The tense after `what if` follows the same reality-distance principles as other conditionals. `What if it rains?` presents a real possibility. `What if you could live anywhere?` invites a more remote hypothetical world. `What if we had missed the flight?` imagines an unreal past alternative.

## Implied conditions without `if`

English often leaves the condition implicit. `I would help, but I'm busy` means roughly `I would help if I were free`. `Another ten minutes and we would have missed the train` compresses a condition into a noun phrase.

`Without your help, I couldn't have finished` means `If I hadn't had your help, I couldn't have finished`. `But for your help` expresses the same idea in more formal style.

This matters because advanced speakers do not always announce conditionals with `if`. The counterfactual relationship can be carried by `would`, context, and a phrase that supplies the missing condition.

## Conditional inversion

Formal English can omit `if` and invert the subject with certain auxiliaries.

`Had I known, I would have called` means `If I had known, I would have called`.

`Were I to accept the offer, I would need to move` is a formal version of `If I were to accept the offer...`.

`Should you need help, please contact us` is a formal and polite version of `If you should need help...` or simply `If you need help...`.

These forms are common enough in contracts, formal emails, speeches, and polished writing that a strong learner should recognize them. In ordinary American conversation, the versions with `if` are usually more natural.

## `Wish` and `if only`: grammar for reality we want to be different

`Wish` uses many of the same distancing patterns as conditionals.

`I wish I had more time` uses a past form for an unreal present desire. The factual background is that I do not have enough time.

`I wish I knew the answer` means I do not know it now. `I wish I were taller` means I am not taller.

For regret about the past, English moves one layer further back: `I wish I had studied harder`, `I wish we hadn't left so early`. The past perfect marks the desired alternative to a completed past reality.

For behavior that the speaker wants to change, `wish + would` is common: `I wish you would stop interrupting me`, `I wish it would stop raining`. This often carries frustration or desire for a future change.

`Wish + would` is usually unnatural when the subject controls a simple personal state that should instead use a remote past form. `I wish I would know the answer` is not standard for `I wish I knew the answer`.

`If only` expresses the same kinds of unreal desire with stronger emotion: `If only I had more time`, `If only I had listened to you`.

## `Would rather` and preference about another person's action

`I'd rather leave now` uses a bare infinitive after `would rather` to express the speaker's own preference.

When the preference concerns another subject, English often uses a past form for present or future preference: `I'd rather you came tomorrow`, `I'd rather he didn't mention it`.

Again, the past form does not mean past time. It creates modal distance from the current reality and frames the desired alternative.

For a past regretful preference, English can use past perfect: `I'd rather you had told me earlier` means I wish you had told me earlier, but you did not.

## `It's time` with a past form

`It's time we left` means the appropriate time to leave is now. The past form `left` creates a mildly remote or overdue sense, not past time.

`It's time to leave` is more direct and neutral. `It's about time we left` often suggests that the action is already late and can carry impatience.

This construction is another clue that English past morphology has a broader function than locating events before now.

## Degrees of hypothetical distance

Compare `If you need help, call me`, `If you should need help, call me`, and `If you needed help, you could call me`.

The first presents need as an open practical possibility. The second adds formal tentativeness. The third creates a more hypothetical or distanced scenario. The grammar lets speakers adjust stance without changing the core event vocabulary.

Similarly, `If he comes, we'll start`, `If he came, we'd start`, and `If he had come, we'd have started` move from open possibility to remote present/future hypothesis to unreal past counterfactual.

The system is coherent because each step increases distance from the speaker's current factual world.

## Common errors come from confusing time with reality

`If I will have time, I will call you` is usually wrong because `will` is unnecessary in the ordinary condition. Standard English says `If I have time, I'll call you`.

`If I would be rich, I would travel` is usually wrong because the condition needs the remote past form: `If I were rich, I would travel`.

`I wish I would be taller` is unnatural for a current state. `I wish I were taller` expresses the unreal present desire.

`If I knew yesterday, I would tell you` mismatches the temporal relationship if the speaker means an unreal past. `If I had known yesterday, I would have told you` correctly builds a past counterfactual.

The solution is not to memorize more isolated rules. Ask whether the condition is open, remote, or already contrary to fact, and ask what time each part belongs to.

## The main mental model

Conditionals create alternative worlds. Simple present forms keep the world close to ordinary reality. Past forms often create distance from present or future reality. Past perfect forms create distance from a completed past reality. `Would`, `could`, and `might` describe what follows inside that imagined world.

Once you understand that English uses tense morphology to express both time and modal distance, conditionals, wishes, polite requests, and hypothetical statements stop looking like unrelated exceptions.
