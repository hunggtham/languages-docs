# Register, the Subjunctive, and Formal Choices

Advanced grammar is not mainly about finding rarer constructions. It is about controlling the relationship between meaning, context, and register. A sentence can be grammatical and still be the wrong choice for a text message, a meeting, a legal notice, a research paper, or a speech.

Register is the variety of language associated with a situation, relationship, and communicative purpose. Informal American conversation allows contractions, phrasal verbs, fragments, direct questions, and personal wording. Professional prose often becomes more explicit and less conversational. Academic or legal writing may use denser noun phrases, impersonal structures, and specialized conventions. A native-like speaker shifts between these without assuming that formal language is automatically better language.

## The American mandative subjunctive

One of the clearest grammatical differences between American and British preferences appears after words that express recommendation, necessity, demand, or importance.

American English naturally says `I recommend that she apply again`, `It is essential that every applicant submit the form`, and `They insisted that he be present`.

The verbs `apply`, `submit`, and `be` are in the plain base form. They do not take third-person `-s`, and `be` remains `be` rather than becoming `is` or `are`. Traditional grammar calls this the mandative subjunctive because the clause expresses something desired, required, recommended, or necessary rather than a plain factual assertion.

A precise grammatical description is that the subjunctive uses the plain form of the verb in certain irrealis or mandative environments. In child-level terms, the verb temporarily stops showing ordinary present-tense agreement because the clause is not saying “this is what happens”; it is saying “this is what should happen.”

Common triggers include `recommend`, `suggest`, `request`, `require`, `demand`, `insist`, `propose`, and expressions such as `it is important that`, `it is necessary that`, `it is essential that`, and `it is vital that`.

British English often uses `should + base verb` in the same environment: `It is essential that every applicant should submit the form.` This is grammatical in American English too, but the bare subjunctive is especially characteristic of formal American usage.

## `Suggest` is not the same as `advise`

`Suggest` causes frequent learner errors because its grammar differs from verbs such as `tell` and `advise`.

Natural American English says `I suggest taking a taxi` or `I suggest that we take a taxi`. Standard English does not normally say `I suggest you to take a taxi`.

`Advise` can take a person plus infinitive: `I advised him to wait`. `Tell` can too: `I told him to wait`.

These patterns are lexical properties of the verbs. Advanced fluency requires learning the complementation pattern as part of the word itself, not assuming all recommendation verbs accept the same grammar.

## The subjunctive `were`

English also preserves a special `were` form in remote hypothetical clauses: `If I were you`, `I wish she were here`, `He talks as if he were the owner`.

In casual American speech, `was` is widely heard with first- and third-person singular subjects, especially outside fixed expressions. `If I was wrong, I'm sorry` can also be a real-condition sentence meaning “if it turns out that I was wrong.” `If I were wrong, the result would be different` clearly builds a hypothetical alternative.

The distinction is useful because `were` marks unreality more explicitly. In formal writing and in the fixed expression `If I were you`, it remains the safest standard choice.

## Fixed subjunctive expressions

Several expressions preserve older subjunctive patterns: `God forbid`, `so be it`, `be that as it may`, `come what may`, `suffice it to say`, and `far be it from me`.

These forms are important mainly for recognition and register. `Be that as it may` means roughly “even if that is accepted, the following point still stands.” It is formal and somewhat rhetorical. In ordinary conversation, `even so`, `still`, or `that may be true, but...` is usually more natural.

`So be it` means the speaker accepts an outcome, often reluctantly: `If that's the final decision, so be it.` It is concise but marked; using it in a routine office chat can sound dramatic.

The lesson is not to collect antique expressions. It is to recognize what they mean and to understand why a native speaker may choose a simpler equivalent in normal conversation.

## Formal conditionals without `if`

Formal English can create conditionals through inversion: `Should you need assistance, contact support`; `Were the company to change its policy, we would review the agreement`; `Had I known earlier, I would have acted differently`.

These are structurally elegant because the auxiliary itself signals the conditional relationship. They are common in formal letters, policies, speeches, and polished prose.

In everyday American speech, `If you need help...`, `If the company changed...`, and `If I had known...` are usually more natural.

The advanced skill is knowing both versions and choosing according to register. Using the inverted form in a casual conversation does not make the English more native-like; it usually makes it more ceremonious.

## Formal concession

Concession means acknowledging one point while asserting another that survives despite it.

Everyday English uses `although`, `even though`, `though`, `but`, and `still`: `Although the plan is expensive, it may be worth it`.

More formal or literary English can use structures such as `Much as I appreciate the offer, I have to decline`, `Unlikely though it may seem, the plan could work`, or `Try as we might, we couldn't reproduce the bug`.

These constructions front the concessive element and create a compact rhetorical rhythm. They are useful in essays, speeches, and literary prose, but they are not the default way Americans talk to coworkers or friends.

`For all` can also create concession: `For all his experience, he made a basic mistake.` The meaning is roughly “despite all his experience.”

## Formal prepositional and linking choices

American English offers several ways to express the same logical relationship at different levels of formality.

`About` is ordinary and conversational. `Regarding`, `concerning`, and `with respect to` are more formal. `As for` often changes topic in speech or writing. `As to` is more formal and commonly appears before a noun or embedded question: `As to whether the plan will work, the evidence is mixed.`

`Because of` is neutral. `Due to` is common in modern American English and widely used before noun phrases, though older style guides tried to restrict it more narrowly. `Owing to` is more strongly associated with British or formal usage.

`If` is neutral. `In the event that` is formal and common in policies or contingency documentation. `Provided that` is formal and conditional. `As long as` is more conversational.

A strong writer chooses the shortest form that fits the context. `In the event that the server becomes unavailable` may be appropriate in a disaster-recovery policy. In a team message, `If the server goes down` is clearer.

## Formal vocabulary does not automatically create formal grammar

Learners sometimes replace everyday verbs with Latinate vocabulary and assume the sentence becomes advanced. Native formal writing depends more on precision and information structure than on difficult words.

`We need to fix this` is direct and natural. `It is necessary to rectify this issue` is more formal, but it may also be heavier than the context requires.

Likewise, `use` is often better than `utilize`, `help` may be better than `facilitate`, and `before` is often better than `prior to`. American professional style generally values clarity over inflated diction.

Advanced grammar should therefore reduce unnecessary complexity, not increase it.

## Nominal style versus verbal style

Formal writing often turns actions into nouns: `We analyzed the data` becomes `An analysis of the data was conducted`.

The second sentence is not automatically better. Nominalization can help when the action itself becomes a topic: `The analysis revealed three patterns. This analysis was later repeated.` It can also create dense, impersonal prose when overused.

American plain-language guidance usually prefers strong verbs when the actor and action matter: `The team analyzed the data` is clearer than `An analysis of the data was conducted by the team`.

Later lessons examine nominalization in more detail, but the register principle starts here: use noun-heavy grammar when it organizes information, not when it merely makes the sentence sound formal.

## Personal and impersonal framing

Conversation naturally uses personal subjects: `I think`, `we found`, `you can see`.

Academic and formal writing sometimes uses impersonal structures to shift focus from the writer to the proposition: `It appears that...`, `It is widely believed that...`, `There is evidence that...`, `The results suggest that...`.

Modern academic English does not ban `I` or `we`; conventions vary by field. The important question is what deserves grammatical focus.

`I think the new method is better` foregrounds the writer. `The results suggest that the new method is more effective` foregrounds evidence. The second is stronger not because it is impersonal, but because it identifies the basis for the claim.

## Contractions and formality

Contractions such as `I'm`, `don't`, `can't`, `we've`, and `it's` are completely normal in American speech and common in informal writing.

Professional emails often use contractions because they sound natural and approachable: `I don't think we'll need another meeting` is ordinary business English.

Very formal contracts, policies, and some academic styles may avoid contractions. But writing every professional email as `I do not believe that we will require...` can sound unnecessarily stiff.

Register control means knowing that full forms are not more grammatical. They simply create a different level of formality or emphasis.

## Phrasal verbs and formal alternatives

American conversation strongly favors phrasal verbs: `find out`, `set up`, `carry out`, `put off`, `look into`, `bring up`.

Formal writing may prefer single-word alternatives when they are more precise: `investigate` for `look into`, `postpone` for `put off`, `establish` or `configure` for certain meanings of `set up`.

But many phrasal verbs are perfectly acceptable in professional prose. `Carry out an experiment` and `set up an account` are standard. Replacing every phrasal verb with a longer Latinate word can make English less natural.

British and American English also differ in the frequency of particular phrasal verbs and lexical alternatives. For example, Americans commonly say `fill out a form`, while British speakers often say `fill in a form`. Americans say `check off` or `check` an item; British English commonly uses `tick` an item.

These lexical differences belong to register and variety, not just vocabulary lists.

## The professional American default

For most workplace communication, the best default is neither casual slang nor highly formal syntax. It is clear General American English with direct verbs, natural contractions where appropriate, explicit logical relationships, and enough politeness to fit the relationship.

`Could you send me the updated file by noon?` is usually better workplace English than either `Send me the file` or `Would you be so kind as to furnish me with the revised document at your earliest convenience?`

The first is direct, polite, specific, and native-like.

## The main mental model

Register is a choice about social and textual distance. The subjunctive, formal inversion, concessive patterns, impersonal framing, nominalization, contractions, and lexical choices all contribute to that distance.

Advanced American English is not the ability to sound formal all the time. It is the ability to sound appropriately formal, appropriately conversational, and appropriately precise without losing clarity.
