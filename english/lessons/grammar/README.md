# English Grammar — A Natural American English Learning Path

This grammar library is meant to be read, not scanned like a formula sheet. The old band-based files were useful as coverage checklists, but they encouraged a learner to jump from one pattern to another before building a stable mental model. The new curriculum follows the way English sentences are actually constructed: first you learn what a sentence is doing, then how English marks reference and time, then how speakers connect ideas, and only after that do you move into compression, emphasis, register, and advanced information structure.

The default variety throughout this library is contemporary General American English. When British English uses a different word, form, or preference that a learner is likely to meet, the difference is folded directly into the explanation instead of being isolated in a separate comparison table. For example, American English normally says `I have a car` and often uses the simple past in places where British English strongly prefers the present perfect, while British English uses `have got` more frequently in everyday possession statements. These are treated as choices with context, not as competing rules.

The explanations are deliberately layered inside the prose. A grammatical term is first given its precise meaning, then immediately restated in plain language, often with the sort of explanation you could give to a child. This keeps technical accuracy without forcing you to leave the page to understand the terminology. Short usage definitions are phrased in the style of major learner dictionaries and grammar references, but the surrounding explanation is rewritten to show why native speakers choose one form over another.

The reading order begins in `01-foundations/`. Start with `01-how-an-english-sentence-works.md`, then continue in file-number order. Do not treat the file boundaries as proficiency bands. They are conceptual boundaries: sentence structure comes before reference, reference comes before time, and time comes before more complex clause relationships.

The library aims to be self-contained. Each lesson explains the form, the meaning, the speaker's point of view, common alternatives, frequent mistakes, and the American-versus-British differences that materially affect understanding. Examples are chosen to sound like ordinary American speech or clear professional writing rather than textbook translation exercises.

As the later branches of this rewrite are added, this same path continues into clause systems, conditionals, modality, information structure, advanced register, ellipsis, and rhetorical grammar. The goal is not to make every sentence complicated. Native control means being able to choose the simplest form that expresses the intended meaning precisely.
