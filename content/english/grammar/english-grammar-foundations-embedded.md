# Noun Clauses and Embedded Questions

So far, most clauses in this curriculum have been treated as complete messages or as modifiers attached to another part of a sentence. English can also take an entire proposition and place it inside a larger sentence as if that proposition were a noun phrase. This is the basic idea behind a noun clause.

A grammar reference describes a noun clause as a subordinate clause that functions in a position where a noun phrase could function. That definition is accurate, but the useful mental model is simpler: English can turn a whole mini-message into one grammatical piece. In `I know the answer`, `the answer` is a noun phrase. In `I know that she left`, the clause `that she left` fills the same general object position after `know`.

This ability is fundamental to real English because people constantly talk not only about things, but about facts, thoughts, questions, possibilities, decisions, and unknown information. `I know him` talks about a person. `I know what he means` talks about the content of an idea. The second sentence is not a special question pattern. It is a larger sentence containing a smaller clause.

## A `that` clause packages a statement as content

After verbs such as `think`, `know`, `believe`, `say`, `hope`, `notice`, `realize`, `remember`, and `hear`, English often uses a clause introduced by `that`.

`I think that the plan will work.`

`She knows that we are here.`

`They realized that the deadline had changed.`

The word `that` here is a complementizer: it marks the following clause as subordinate to the larger sentence. It does not point to an object the way demonstrative `that` does in `that book`.

In ordinary American conversation, `that` is often omitted when the relationship is clear: `I think the plan will work`, `She said she was tired`, `I know you're busy`. The version with `that` is not more correct. Keeping it can make a long sentence easier to parse, create a slight pause, or prevent temporary ambiguity.

This is a useful example of native grammar being partly about processing. A speaker may omit `that` in a short sentence because the listener can understand the structure immediately, but keep it when the embedded clause is long or when several noun phrases appear before it.

## The embedded clause keeps statement word order

The most important principle in this lesson is that an embedded clause is not automatically a question just because it contains a question word.

Compare the direct question:

`Where is she?`

with the embedded question:

`Do you know where she is?`

The outer sentence is the question. Inside it, `where she is` behaves like a noun clause, so it uses ordinary statement order: subject before verb. Standard English does not say `Do you know where is she?`

The same pattern appears in:

`I wonder what he wants.`

`Can you tell me when the meeting starts?`

`Nobody knows why the system failed.`

`She asked how much it cost.`

A child-friendly way to understand this is that only the outside question gets the special question shape. The question inside has been packed into the larger sentence, so it settles back into normal word order.

## `do`, `does`, and `did` disappear inside embedded questions

Direct questions with ordinary lexical verbs often require `do` support:

`Where does he work?`

But the embedded version is:

`Do you know where he works?`

not `where does he work`.

The same applies in the past:

`Why did they leave?`

becomes:

`I don't know why they left.`

The auxiliary `did` existed to create direct-question structure. Once the clause becomes embedded, that operation is no longer needed, so the lexical verb carries the tense normally.

This connection matters because it unifies two topics that are often taught separately. Embedded questions are not a list of exceptions. They follow directly from the difference between a main interrogative clause and a subordinate clause.

## `whether` and `if` package yes-or-no possibilities

A direct yes-or-no question has no `wh` word:

`Is she coming?`

To embed that uncertainty inside another sentence, English commonly uses `whether` or `if`:

`I don't know whether she is coming.`

`I don't know if she is coming.`

Both are natural in ordinary American English in many contexts. `If` is especially common in conversation. `Whether` is useful when the alternatives themselves are important, when the style is more formal, or in grammatical environments where `if` does not work as well.

`Whether` is preferred before an infinitive: `I don't know whether to go.` Standard English does not normally say `I don't know if to go`.

After a preposition, `whether` is also the normal choice: `It depends on whether they agree.` `It depends on if they agree` is heard informally, but careful standard prose strongly favors `whether` after the preposition.

When both alternatives are stated, `whether` is often clearer: `We need to decide whether to stay or leave.`

The expression `whether or not` makes the two possibilities explicit: `I'll go whether or not he comes.` Here the meaning is not uncertainty about one condition; it means the result will be the same under either alternative.

## Question words can represent missing information, not just introduce questions

Words such as `what`, `who`, `where`, `when`, `why`, and `how` often mark a missing element inside an embedded clause.

`I know where she lives.`

The meaning is roughly: I know the answer to the question “Where does she live?” But grammatically, `where she lives` is now the object of `know`.

`Tell me what you need.`

Here `what` represents the thing needed. The whole clause `what you need` functions as the content to be told.

`Who` can be the subject inside the clause: `I don't know who called.` Because the unknown person is already the subject, there is no extra inversion or `do`, just as in the direct subject question `Who called?`

This becomes important in longer sentences. Instead of trying to translate a question word one word at a time, ask what information the entire clause represents.

## `what` can create a fused relative meaning

In sentences such as `I understand what you mean`, the word `what` can be understood as combining the meaning of “the thing that.” Grammars often call this a fused relative construction because the antecedent and relative word are fused together.

`What you said surprised me` means approximately `The thing that you said surprised me.`

`Take what you need` means roughly `Take the things that you need.`

This is different from an ordinary relative clause such as `the thing that you need`, where the noun `thing` is stated separately. The fused construction is extremely common and natural in both speech and writing.

Do not replace `what` with `that` in this structure. `I understand that you mean` does not mean `I understand what you mean`; `that` would need to introduce a complete content clause such as `I understand that you mean well`.

## Noun clauses can be subjects, but English often moves heavy information later

A noun clause can function as the grammatical subject:

`That he refused surprised everyone.`

`What she said was true.`

`Whether we succeed depends on several factors.`

These are grammatical, but a long `that` clause in subject position can feel heavy. English often prefers a dummy `it` construction:

`It surprised everyone that he refused.`

`It is obvious that something went wrong.`

`It seems that the server is down.`

This reflects the information-flow principle developed elsewhere in the curriculum: English often places lighter, familiar structural material earlier and heavier content later. The `it` does not refer to an object; it allows the content clause to appear in a more comfortable final position.

This pattern is especially common in formal and professional English with expressions such as `It is clear that...`, `It is possible that...`, `It appears that...`, and `It is important that...`. Later lessons explain how some of these environments can interact with the American mandative subjunctive.

## Clauses can also follow nouns and adjectives

Content clauses are not limited to verbs. Nouns can carry propositional content:

`the fact that he left`

`the idea that the system can recover automatically`

`the possibility that prices will rise`

The `that` clause tells us the content of the fact, idea, or possibility. This is different from a relative clause. In `the fact that surprised me`, `that` is part of a relative clause and refers back to `fact`. In `the fact that he left`, the clause expresses what the fact is.

Adjectives can also be followed by clauses:

`I'm glad that you came.`

`I'm sure that she knows.`

`We were surprised that nobody complained.`

The clause completes the meaning of the adjective by identifying the proposition connected to the emotion or judgment.

## Reporting thoughts and questions uses the same system

Reported speech becomes easier when you see it as clause embedding rather than as a separate transformation exercise.

`She said, “I'm tired.”` can become `She said that she was tired.` The reported proposition is embedded as the complement of `said`.

`She asked, “Where do you live?”` becomes `She asked where I lived.` The direct question becomes an embedded interrogative clause, which is why statement word order appears.

`She asked, “Are you ready?”` becomes `She asked whether I was ready` or `She asked if I was ready.`

Backshifting and changes in pronouns or time expressions depend on viewpoint and context, but the structural core is simple: the message being reported has become one component inside a new sentence.

## Polite questions often use embedding to reduce directness

American English frequently uses an embedded question after a polite frame:

`Could you tell me where the restroom is?`

`Do you know what time the store closes?`

`Would you mind telling me how this works?`

The polite outer clause changes the social force of the sentence. The speaker is grammatically asking whether the listener can tell them, but pragmatically requesting the information itself.

This is why `Could you tell me where is the restroom?` sounds wrong in standard English even though both parts may look like questions. The outer clause already performs the question operation. The embedded information clause keeps subordinate-clause order.

In ordinary conversation, speakers may shorten the frame to `Any idea where she is?` or `Do you know when?` when context makes the missing material recoverable. These are extensions of the same system, not random fragments.

## `I think` and similar clauses can behave like stance frames

In conversation, expressions such as `I think`, `I guess`, `I suppose`, and `I believe` often do more than report a private mental act. They frame the speaker's degree of commitment to the proposition.

`I think he's right` often means roughly “My current judgment is that he's right.” The content `he's right` carries the main informational weight, while `I think` softens or frames the claim.

This helps explain conversational patterns such as `I think so`, `I don't think so`, and the American preference for `I don't think he's coming` rather than the logically possible but much less ordinary `I think he isn't coming`. English often places the negation in the higher clause when the speaker is rejecting the embedded proposition as a belief.

The phenomenon is sometimes called negative raising. You do not need the label to use it naturally. The practical point is that `I don't think...` is one of the normal ways American speakers soften a negative judgment.

## Do not confuse embedded questions with relative clauses

Compare:

`I know where she lives.`

and:

`I know the neighborhood where she lives.`

In the first sentence, `where she lives` is the thing known: an embedded question/content clause. In the second, `where she lives` modifies `the neighborhood`; it is a relative clause.

The words can look almost identical, but their jobs differ. A reliable test is to ask what grammatical slot the clause fills. If the whole clause is the object or subject of another expression, it behaves like a noun clause. If it describes a noun already present, it behaves like a relative clause.

## American and British English are mostly aligned here

The central grammar of noun clauses and embedded questions is shared across major standard varieties. Both American and British English use statement order inside embedded questions and both use `whether` and `if` in many of the same environments.

Differences are mostly preferences of frequency and style rather than competing systems. American conversation often omits complementizer `that` freely after common reporting and thinking verbs. British English does the same. In both varieties, formal prose may retain `that` when it improves clarity.

The production rule for this curriculum remains simple: use natural American phrasing, but recognize that the clause architecture itself is international standard English.

## What to carry into the core lessons

A clause does not have to be the whole sentence. English can package a proposition, an unknown piece of information, or a yes-or-no alternative as one grammatical unit inside a larger structure.

When the embedded unit comes from a question, do not preserve direct-question inversion. `I wonder where he is`, `Tell me what she wants`, and `Do you know whether they agreed?` all use subordinate-clause order inside the larger sentence.

This completes an important part of the foundation: you can now build a clause, connect clauses, use them conversationally, and place one clause inside another. The core lessons can therefore focus less on sentence assembly and more on viewpoint, reality, agency, information packaging, and speaker choice.
