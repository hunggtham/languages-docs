# How an English Sentence Works

English grammar becomes much easier when you stop seeing a sentence as a row of slots to fill and start seeing it as a small message built around a relationship. In a basic statement such as `Maria works in Seoul`, the sentence names a participant, `Maria`, and says something about that participant, `works in Seoul`. Traditional grammar calls `Maria` the subject and `works in Seoul` the predicate. A more technical definition of the subject is the noun phrase that has the central grammatical relationship with the verb. In simpler language, it is usually the person or thing the sentence is built around.

The verb is the center of the predicate. A verb expresses an action, event, process, or state. That definition sounds abstract, but the practical idea is simple: the verb tells you what is happening or what condition exists. Compare `Mina runs`, `Mina became tired`, and `Mina knows the answer`. `runs` describes an action, `became` describes a change of state, and `knows` describes a state of mind. All three are verbs even though only one looks like a physical action.

English word order matters because English does not mark grammatical roles with many endings. In ordinary statements, the language strongly prefers subject before verb and, when the verb takes an object, object after the verb: `Daniel opened the door.` The subject is Daniel, the action is opened, and the object is the door. If you reverse the nouns and say `The door opened Daniel`, the grammar now says something completely different, even if the vocabulary is unchanged. Think of word order as road lanes. English gives each major part of the sentence a lane so the listener can understand the relationship quickly.

## The special verb `be`

`Be` is unusual because it often connects the subject to a description or identity instead of describing an action. In `I am tired`, `am` links `I` with the adjective `tired`. In `She is a designer`, `is` links `she` with the noun phrase `a designer`. Grammar books often call this a linking verb or copular verb. The important mental model is that `be` works like an equals sign or a bridge: `she = a designer`, `the room = quiet`, `they = at home`.

Because `be` already behaves like an auxiliary verb, it does not need `do` to make a question or a negative. We say `She is ready`, `She is not ready`, and `Is she ready?` Native speakers do not say `Does she be ready?` in ordinary standard English. If you explain this to a child, the shortcut is: `be` can move by itself. Other ordinary verbs usually need the helper `do`.

The present forms are `am`, `is`, and `are`. `I` goes with `am`; `he`, `she`, `it`, and a singular noun usually go with `is`; `you`, `we`, `they`, and plural nouns go with `are`. In conversation, contractions are normal: `I'm`, `you're`, `he's`, `she's`, `it's`, `we're`, and `they're`. The negative forms `isn't` and `aren't` are common. Standard American English does not normally use `amn't`; speakers usually say `I'm not`.

`Be` also appears with place expressions: `The keys are on the table.` The phrase `on the table` is not an adjective, but it still completes the description of where the keys are. This matters because English often uses `be` to place things in space, time, or a situation: `The meeting is at three`, `My office is downtown`, `The problem is in the database`.

## Ordinary verbs and the helper `do`

With most ordinary verbs, present-tense negatives and questions use `do`. Compare `You work here`, `You do not work here`, and `Do you work here?` With third-person singular subjects such as `he`, `she`, or `the system`, the helper changes to `does`: `She works here`, `She doesn't work here`, `Does she work here?`

Notice what happens to the main verb. Once `does` carries the tense and agreement, the main verb returns to its base form. We say `Does she work?`, not `Does she works?` The easiest way to understand this is that English only needs one word in this verb group to carry the grammatical marking. `Does` is already doing that job.

The same principle appears in the past: `He worked`, `He didn't work`, `Did he work?` The word `did` carries past tense, so the following verb is again the base form. `Did he worked?` is a common learner error because the speaker marks the past twice.

American conversation frequently contracts `do not`, `does not`, and `did not` to `don't`, `doesn't`, and `didn't`. Full forms are not more correct; they are simply more emphatic or more formal in some contexts. `I do not agree` can sound deliberately firm, while `I don't agree` is the ordinary conversational choice.

## Objects and complements

Some verbs can stand alone: `The baby slept.` Others normally expect another participant: `She bought a laptop.` The noun phrase after `bought` is the object. A dictionary-style grammatical definition of an object is a noun phrase that is selected by a verb and typically represents the person or thing affected by the action. Put simply, it is often what the action lands on.

This is why `She bought` can be grammatical in a special context but normally feels incomplete if the listener expects to know what she bought. Verbs differ in the complements they allow. `Sleep` normally does not take a direct object, while `buy` often does. Learning a verb therefore means learning not only its dictionary meaning but also the sentence patterns it naturally enters.

Some verbs take two objects: `She gave me the key.` Here `me` is the recipient and `the key` is the thing transferred. American English also allows `She gave the key to me.` The two structures are close in meaning, but the choice can shift the focus. The final position often carries newer or heavier information, so `She gave the key to the new engineer from Boston` is usually smoother than placing that long phrase before `the key`.

A complement is broader than an object. In `She became tired`, `tired` completes the meaning of `became`, but it is not an object. In `They elected him president`, `president` tells us what role `him` acquired. You do not need to memorize every label immediately. What matters is seeing that English verbs create different frames, and the rest of the sentence fills those frames.

## Pronouns and why case still matters

English has lost most of the case endings found in older forms of the language, but pronouns still show a small case system. We use subject forms such as `I`, `he`, `she`, `we`, and `they` before the verb, and object forms such as `me`, `him`, `her`, `us`, and `them` after verbs and prepositions. `She called me` and `I called her` show the contrast clearly.

After a preposition, American English normally uses the object form: `with me`, `for him`, `between us`, `to them`. A sentence such as `between you and I` is common enough to be heard, but careful standard usage prefers `between you and me` because `between` is a preposition.

After forms of `be`, traditional school grammar once strongly promoted forms such as `It is I`. In modern American English, ordinary speakers overwhelmingly say `It's me`, `That's him`, and `This is her`. The older subject-form version survives in highly formal or stylized contexts. This is a good example of why native usage matters: grammatical tradition and current natural speech are not always identical.

Possessive forms also come in two patterns. `My`, `your`, `his`, `her`, `our`, and `their` must normally be followed by a noun: `my phone`, `their apartment`. `Mine`, `yours`, `his`, `hers`, `ours`, and `theirs` can stand alone: `That phone is mine.` Saying `mine phone` mixes the two systems.

## Statements, negatives, and questions are transformations of the same idea

A useful way to learn English is to start with a neutral statement and then watch how English changes it. Take `You are tired.` The negative is `You are not tired.` The yes/no question is `Are you tired?` Because `be` can move by itself, the structure changes directly.

Now take `You work here.` The negative is `You do not work here.` The question is `Do you work here?` English inserts `do` because an ordinary lexical verb does not normally move to the front in modern English.

For information questions, add a question word such as `what`, `where`, `when`, `why`, `who`, or `how`: `Where do you work?`, `Why is she upset?`, `When did they arrive?` The question word marks the missing piece of information. You can imagine the listener filling a blank: `You work ____` becomes `Where do you work?`

There is one important exception. When `who` or `what` itself is the subject, English does not normally add `do`: `Who called you?` The unknown person is already occupying the subject position. Compare that with `Who did you call?`, where `who` is the object and `you` remains the subject.

## Dummy subjects: when English needs a grammatical placeholder

English generally requires an explicit subject, even when the meaning does not naturally contain a person or thing. This is why we say `It is raining`, not simply `Is raining`. The `it` in weather expressions does not refer to a specific object. Grammarians call it a dummy or expletive subject. A child-friendly explanation is that English likes to keep the subject chair filled, even when no real person is sitting in it.

The same happens in `It is important to rest` and `It is hard to explain the problem`. The real conceptual content comes later, but `it` gives the sentence a normal English shape.

`There` can also work as a grammatical placeholder in existential sentences: `There is a problem`, `There are two people outside`. Here `there` does not necessarily mean a location. The construction presents something as existing or appearing in the conversation. If someone asks `What's in the room?`, `There is a table by the window` introduces the table as new information.

This is different from possession. `There is a laptop on the desk` means that a laptop exists in that location. `I have a laptop` means that the laptop belongs to me or is available to me. Learners often translate both through a single word meaning “have” or “exist,” but English keeps these functions separate.

In informal American speech, you may hear `There's two people outside`, with singular `there's` before a plural noun. This is common conversationally, especially when the speaker begins the sentence before fully planning the noun phrase. In careful writing, `There are two people outside` remains the standard form.

## Building longer noun phrases without losing the sentence

The subject and object are often more than one word. In `The new engineer from our Seoul office fixed the problem`, the full subject is `The new engineer from our Seoul office`. The core noun is `engineer`, while `the`, `new`, and `from our Seoul office` help identify which engineer.

When sentences become longer, find the head noun and the main verb first. This prevents you from mistaking a noun inside a prepositional phrase for the subject. In `The list of new features is long`, the subject is `the list`, so the verb is singular `is`. The plural noun `features` belongs inside `of new features`; it does not control agreement.

This simple habit becomes essential later when you read sentences with relative clauses, participle clauses, and academic noun phrases. Advanced grammar is not a different language. It is the same basic sentence architecture with more information attached to the major parts.

## The main idea to carry forward

A good sentence is not created by choosing a tense first. It starts with a relationship: who or what the message is about, what state or event you want to express, and what other participants or descriptions the verb requires. Once that relationship is clear, English grammar provides tools to mark time, certainty, reference, focus, and connection.

If you can look at a sentence and identify its subject, its main verb, and the role of the material around that verb, you already have the foundation for every later lesson in this library.
